# Orbital Exchange Design Assumptions

**Status:** working project assumptions  
**Last updated:** 2026-08-26

This document defines the target market that `open-ipo` is designing toward.

The **Orbital Exchange** is a nascent, yet-to-be-launched space-based marketplace intended for the listing, trading, and execution of securities of **non-terrestrial companies**.

Nothing in this file should be read as an adopted Orbital Exchange rule unless the exchange later publishes it as such. The purpose of this repository is to make the design questions explicit early enough that agents, issuers, market operators, investors, engineers, lawyers, accountants, and space operators can improve them in public.

---

## 1. Market purpose

The Orbital Exchange should make a new class of enterprise legible to public capital markets: companies whose value materially depends on economic activity beyond Earth’s surface.

That could include businesses built around:

- orbital infrastructure,
- communications and sensing,
- in-space servicing and logistics,
- in-space manufacturing,
- power and compute infrastructure,
- habitats and stations,
- resource prospecting or utilization,
- autonomous spacecraft and robotic systems,
- agent-operated space services,
- and future categories that do not fit terrestrial industry classifications.

The exchange should not merely reproduce a terrestrial exchange inside a spacecraft. Its market design should recognize that the underlying issuers, assets, communications paths, operational evidence, and failure modes may themselves be non-terrestrial.

---

## 2. Working definition: non-terrestrial company

For `open-ipo`, a **non-terrestrial company** is an issuer whose enterprise value materially depends on assets, infrastructure, operations, services, resources, or autonomous economic activity beyond Earth’s surface.

This is a project definition, not a legal classification or adopted listing standard.

A future Orbital Exchange eligibility test could consider evidence such as:

- percentage of assets located off Earth,
- percentage of revenue generated by off-Earth activity,
- percentage of operating cost tied to off-Earth infrastructure,
- dependence on orbital or deep-space assets,
- mission-critical autonomous systems operating beyond Earth,
- or whether the company’s core product could exist without non-terrestrial operations.

The goal should be to avoid both extremes: excluding legitimate early space businesses because most employees remain on Earth, or allowing ordinary terrestrial companies to qualify through superficial space exposure.

## 3. Proposed market layers

A complete Orbital Exchange stack will eventually need explicit standards for at least six layers.

### 3.1 Admission and listing

Questions include:

- What makes an issuer eligible as non-terrestrial?
- What financial, governance, operating-history, liquidity, and ownership thresholds apply?
- Which off-Earth assets or operations must be verified before admission?
- What continuing conditions can trigger deficiency, suspension, or delisting?

### 3.2 Disclosure

Questions include:

- What must issuers disclose about spacecraft, payloads, missions, orbital assets, licenses, autonomy, counterparties, and operational dependencies?
- Which telemetry or machine-readable evidence should accompany narrative disclosure?
- How should issuers report loss of contact, mission degradation, collision risk, launch failure, loss of licensing, or loss of a material space asset?

### 3.3 Trading and execution

Because the marketplace is intended to be space-based, market design should explicitly address:

- where matching or execution occurs,
- which node or system is authoritative,
- communications latency between Earth and orbital infrastructure,
- deterministic ordering of messages,
- authoritative timestamps,
- network partitions,
- stale orders,
- trading halts,
- degraded-mode operation,
- and recovery after temporary loss of connectivity.

A terrestrial market can often treat network topology as infrastructure. An orbital market may have to treat it as part of the rulebook.

### 3.4 Clearing, settlement, and custody

The exchange will need a defined model for:

- trade finality,
- asset and cash settlement,
- custody,
- key management,
- failed settlement,
- reconciliation across Earth-space links,
- corporate actions,
- ownership records,
- and recovery when one side of the market is temporarily unreachable.

The system should make a clear distinction between **execution location**, **recordkeeping location**, **custody location**, and **legal situs** rather than assuming they are the same place.

### 3.5 Surveillance and market integrity

A public market should be able to detect and investigate manipulation, abusive trading, compromised agents, erroneous autonomous strategies, and failures in market infrastructure.

Orbital market surveillance may need to reason across:

- delayed message arrival,
- multiple clocks,
- autonomous trading agents,
- intermittent connectivity,
- geographically and physically distributed infrastructure,
- and the possibility that operational events affecting an issuer are first observable through telemetry rather than a press release.

### 3.6 Continuing operations and resilience

The exchange itself should have standards for:

- redundant execution infrastructure,
- ground fallback,
- orbital fallback,
- failover authority,
- loss of communications,
- space-weather events,
- collision or debris events,
- power constraints,
- cyber incidents,
- software or model failures,
- and safe market shutdown/restart.

A space-based exchange should be able to explain what remains authoritative when its ideal architecture is unavailable.

## 4. Time is a market-structure primitive

In an orbital market, “when did this order arrive?” can become a physically distributed systems question.

The exchange should eventually specify:

- authoritative time sources,
- acceptable clock drift,
- timestamp precision,
- sequencing rules,
- treatment of delayed messages,
- treatment of duplicated messages,
- treatment of messages received after a halt or state transition,
- and the evidence retained to reconstruct market events.

The design goal should be **reconstructability**: after any material event, an independent reviewer should be able to establish the sequence of market states and participant actions.

---

## 5. Location is a disclosure primitive

For both the exchange and its issuers, physical location can affect risk.

Potentially material location facts include:

- orbit or operating region,
- ground-station dependence,
- launch provider and launch site,
- command-and-control paths,
- jurisdiction of key operating entities,
- custody and recordkeeping locations,
- physical concentration of critical infrastructure,
- and whether an asset can be serviced, replaced, deorbited, or recovered.

The future rulebook should distinguish between location that is merely descriptive and location that changes economic, legal, operational, or market risk.

---

## 6. Telemetry can become financial evidence

For a non-terrestrial issuer, investors may need evidence about operating assets that cannot be physically inspected on demand.

A future Orbital Exchange could explore standards for signed or otherwise verifiable telemetry covering material operating facts such as:

- asset status,
- availability,
- uptime,
- energy state,
- mission phase,
- payload operation,
- service capacity,
- loss of contact,
- collision avoidance events,
- or other metrics tied directly to revenue or asset value.

Telemetry should not automatically become public simply because it exists. The challenge is to identify which operational facts are material, verifiable, safe to disclose, and useful to investors.

---

## 7. Agents are native participants, not an edge case

The Orbital Exchange should assume from the beginning that agents may participate throughout the stack:

- issuer operations,
- disclosure preparation,
- compliance monitoring,
- investor analysis,
- trading,
- execution routing,
- surveillance,
- reconciliation,
- and market operations.

That means the rulebook should define responsibility and evidence when autonomous systems act.

A useful design principle is:

```text
autonomous action
      ↓
defined authority
      ↓
observable policy boundary
      ↓
cryptographically or operationally attributable event
      ↓
reconstructable audit trail
      ↓
accountable legal / governance owner
```

Autonomy should increase automation, not ambiguity.

---

## 8. Terrestrial markets are precedent, not destiny

Existing public markets provide mature answers to many problems the Orbital Exchange will also face: issuer disclosure, governance, auditability, market integrity, listing eligibility, continuing obligations, trading controls, settlement, and investor protection.

`open-ipo` should study those systems closely.

But it should not assume their implementation choices remain optimal when:

- issuer assets are off Earth,
- operations depend on space communications,
- agents control material processes,
- the exchange infrastructure itself is space-based,
- and market evidence can include machine-generated operational data.

The correct question is not “How do we copy Nasdaq or NYSE into orbit?”

It is:

> **Which public-market functions remain essential, and what is the best architecture for performing them in a non-terrestrial economy?**

---

## 9. Open design questions

1. Where must the authoritative matching engine physically operate for the venue to be meaningfully “orbital”?
2. Can execution remain valid during temporary Earth-space network partition?
3. What timestamp and sequencing model is fair when participants experience different physical latencies?
4. What is the exchange’s legal jurisdiction if key infrastructure operates in orbit?
5. What systems are authoritative when orbital and ground records temporarily disagree?
6. How should the venue distinguish ordinary volatility from price discovery after a mission event?
7. Which issuer telemetry can support disclosure without creating security or operational risk?
8. What does a trading halt mean when messages are already in flight?
9. How should autonomous trading agents identify themselves and their responsible principals?
10. What evidence is required to reconstruct an autonomous market action?
11. How should custody and settlement operate across intermittent connectivity?
12. What minimum fallback mode preserves market integrity after loss of orbital infrastructure?
13. Which categories of non-terrestrial company belong on the venue at launch?
14. When does a terrestrial company become sufficiently non-terrestrial to qualify?
15. What new listing standards are necessary for physical space assets whose useful lives are tied to mission duration?

These questions are not reasons to delay the project. They are the initial specification.
